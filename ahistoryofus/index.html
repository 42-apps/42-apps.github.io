A History of Us

We are one species, born in Africa. Watch our ancestors spread from a single cradle to every corner of the Earth — and see who they were, in their bones and their genes.

  • ▶Press play. Time runs left→right. Regions glow with the genetic lineage of whoever lived there; watch the colours branch and spread.
  • ➜Follow the journeys. Animated arcs trace each great migration — out of Africa, across the seas to Australia, into the Americas, across the Pacific.
  • 🧭Tap a site or region. Blombos, Lake Mungo, Denisova… each discovery and people opens a card with its story, DNA and living descendants.
  • ❄️Climate drives it all. Ice sheets spread and melt, seas fall to open land bridges, and lakes dry up — the gauge shows how hot or cold it was, and why people moved.

A History of Us

A living atlas of the human journey. Drag the timeline to travel from 300,000 years ago — when Homo sapiens was confined to Africa — to today, and watch our ancestors people the world. Each lit region is coloured by the deep genetic lineage of the people who lived there then; animated arcs trace the great migrations; dots mark the fossils, art and sites that tell the story; and the Ice-Age world of land bridges, vanished lakes and lower seas shifts beneath.

Please read this: deep prehistory is uncertain and fast-moving. Every date, range, boundary and population here is an approximation, often debated, and the regions are coarse "where-ish" zones, not borders. Genetics are reduced to a few characteristic lineages (mtDNA / Y-DNA haplogroups + notable ancient-DNA samples + living descendants) — real ancestry is a blend. Ice sheets, sea level and temperature are simplified sketches of the glacial cycles, and the other humans (Neanderthals, Denisovans and their cousins) are shown as rough ranges ending at approximate extinction dates. The deeper the past, the fuzzier it all becomes.

On the "cradle": we open in Africa and highlight a proposed southern-African homeland (the Makgadikgadi wetland, mtDNA L0). That single-origin idea is contested; most researchers now favour a pan-African origin — H. sapiens emerging from populations spread across the whole continent (the oldest fossils, Jebel Irhoud, are in Morocco). We show both.

Built on the science of palaeoanthropology and archaeogenetics — work by the labs of Pääbo, Reich, Willerslev, Tishkoff and many others; key studies include Hublin et al. 2017 (Jebel Irhoud), Vidal et al. 2022 (Omo), Clarkson et al. 2017 (Madjedbebe), Chan et al. 2019 (southern-African mtDNA), and the Lapita/Polynesian and steppe/Anatolian ancient-DNA literature.
